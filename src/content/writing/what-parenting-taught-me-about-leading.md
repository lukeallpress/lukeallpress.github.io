I've learned many of my most important colleague and manager skills from being a parent.

Working moms, especially, are often treated as primary parents — and they carry the emotional and social skills to balance that, plus the grit of having lived through it. I'm grateful to work with so many of them: people who lead, problem-solve, mentor, organize, and show up, and then go home and do the household work that still falls disproportionately on them.

The numbers back up what's easy to overlook. Globally, women perform the large majority of unpaid care work. In the workplace, motherhood still gets treated as a risk factor in ways fatherhood usually does not — and the bias behind those assumptions shows up in hiring, promotions, flexibility, and quiet judgments about ambition.

I think about that when I think about culture and leadership. The skills we most need from leaders — patience, clear framing, holding multiple priorities at once, staying calm in chaos — are the same skills a lot of parents practice every single day. Naming that, and making room for it, is part of building a workplace worth being in.
